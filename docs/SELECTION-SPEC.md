# Opinion selection specification

The agent selects against this document. The gate enforces the parts of it that
can be enforced mechanically; the rest is judgment, and the judgment is stated
here so it can be audited.

## Who is reading

A company outside New Jersey has been served. Nobody has appeared. The letter
arrives and someone clicks through to a judge page. That reader is three to six
weeks into a case, and what they face over the next ninety days is a decision
about whether to move to dismiss, a question about why they are being sued in
New Jersey at all, possibly a pending order to show cause, and a scheduling
conference in front of a magistrate judge they have never heard of.

They are not going to trial. Many of these cases will never produce a summary
judgment opinion.

The consequence runs through every rule below. The useful opinion is not the
judge's most important ruling in a trade secrets case. It is the judge's ruling
on what this defendant is about to live through. Selection targets the
intersection of early-stage procedure and the six matter types, not the matter
types alone. Taxonomy is necessary and insufficient: run on taxonomy by itself
and you get the wrong opinions in the right categories.

## Priority of postures

Personal jurisdiction under Rule 12(b)(2) sits at the top, and not by a small
margin. Every out-of-state defendant's first question is why New Jersey. A
judge's body of 12(b)(2) opinions answers that question in the court's own
words, is fact-specific enough to be genuinely informative, is entirely neutral
to summarize, and is aggregated nowhere else. The prior research report contains
almost none of it.

Then venue and transfer under section 1404(a) and first-filed disputes. Then TRO
and preliminary injunction practice, which is concentrated in exactly the trade
secret and restrictive covenant matters where this reader is most often the
defendant. Then arbitration and forum selection, then remand after removal, then
Rule 12(b)(6) on the substantive claims. Summary judgment is last, not because
it is unimportant but because this reader is nowhere near it.

Case management sits higher than its prestige suggests, which brings us to the
magistrate judges.

## The magistrate judges are not a fast-follow

The prior report calls the magistrate corpus structurally thinner and treats
those pages as secondary. That is true of published merits opinions and false of
everything this reader needs. In this district the magistrate runs the case.
The recipient will see far more of the magistrate than the district judge in
year one: the scheduling order, discovery disputes by letter, the settlement
conference.

Magistrate output is also almost entirely procedural, which makes it both easy
to summarize and nearly impossible to summarize in a way that characterizes the
judge. The magistrate pages should be as strong as the district judge pages.
The mail piece names both; the site should honor both.

## What comes out

Criminal work goes. The report is thick with sentencing, prisoner civil rights
and habeas. None of it serves a commercial defendant, and a page dominated by it
reads like a criminal defense site.

Mass tort and MDL rulings go for a different reason: impressive and irrelevant.
Those defendants already have counsel.

Specialized statutory work outside the six categories goes — ERISA benefits,
IDEA, False Claims Act, Hatch-Waxman, social security. Interesting, not ours.

## The blockbusters stay, in their own tier

Koons, Khalil, McIver, the assault weapons case, the county line case. These are
what the report leads with and by any journalistic standard they are the
significant opinions. They are also the ones with the most volatile posture and
the highest maintenance cost: as of the last check, three of four are suspended,
each in a different way.

They cannot be dropped. If Bumb's page has no Koons on it, any lawyer who looks
knows immediately that the site is a marketing artifact wearing a reference
costume. The blockbusters are what make the neutrality claim credible, and
credibility is the whole asset.

So two tiers, with different rules.

The significant tier is capped at three per judge and each entry runs sixty
words or fewer: identify the matter in two sentences, state the current posture,
link. It exists for credibility, not conversion. A four-hundred word treatment
of a case that moves every few months is a standing rewrite obligation, and
2,500 words does not compress a 230-page constitutional opinion anyway.

The matter tier carries the business content and takes the full 250 to 500 word
headnote — facts, issues, holding. That length fits a jurisdiction or injunction
opinion exactly. The two tiers exist because one length cannot serve both.

## Maintenance as a selection criterion

Under gate 6a every disturbed-posture entry costs a recurring status check,
forever. Three or four across forty-three judges is fine. Thirty would sink the
automation and turn the review gate into a nuisance that someone eventually
disables.

So bias toward settled opinions: decided, affirmed or unappealed, more than two
years old. A 2022 personal jurisdiction ruling costs nothing to maintain. A case
on a stayed mandate costs a check every month. The gate warns above twelve
disturbed entries across the tree.

## The line not to cross

Do not tally. No grant rates. No "denied dismissal in seven of nine." No counts
of how often a judge sends trade secret cases to arbitration. A tally is a
prediction wearing the costume of a fact, and it is squarely what RPC 8.4(e) and
8.2 are aimed at. Gate 8 blocks the obvious phrasings, but the gate is a
backstop, not the policy.

The quieter trap is selection itself. Choose only the cases where a judge
granted dismissal and the page implies a pattern with no numbers on it at all.
So: select by posture, subject, recency and the availability of a free public
copy. Never by outcome, never by who won.

The selection criteria go on the methodology page, so a reader can verify the
neutrality instead of taking it on faith. Published criteria are also what let
the site be defended if anyone ever asks why a particular case is on a
particular page.

## Thin benches

A procedural filter still leaves the newest judges thin. Kiel and Semper have
short benches. Fais and Silagi have none. Mail will go to defendants in front of
all of them, and a defendant assigned to Judge Kiel would otherwise land on an
empty page.

The fix is structural, not editorial. The practice information block and the
appearing-from-out-of-state material become the spine of every judge page, above
the case list rather than an addendum below it. Those sections are complete for
every judge on day one, they come from the court, and for this reader they may
be the more useful content regardless of how long the bench is.

Do not pad a thin bench with off-taxonomy filler. An honest empty state beats a
sentence that says nothing, and the prior report is full of the second kind.
